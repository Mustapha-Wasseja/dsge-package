Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models are the workhorse of modern macroeconomics. Central banks and international institutions use them to forecast, to work out which shocks have been driving inflation and output, and to ask what would have happened under a different policy. Most people build them in Dynare, which runs on MATLAB or Octave. If the rest of your work is in R, that means moving your data into another language halfway through.

dsge is an R package that covers the whole job: write down a model, solve it, estimate it by maximum likelihood or Bayesian methods, and then look at impulse responses, forecasts and shock decompositions. There is no MATLAB or Octave involved. This post estimates a small New Keynesian model on US data, then shows nonlinear models and importing a Dynare file.

install.packages("dsge")
library(dsge)

A three-equation New Keynesian model

The model has three equations:

  • Phillips curve. Inflation p depends on expected inflation next quarter and on the output gap x.
  • IS curve. The output gap depends on the expected output gap and on the real interest rate, and is pushed around by a demand shock g.
  • Taylor rule. The central bank sets the interest rate r in response to inflation, plus a monetary policy shock u.

Both shocks follow AR(1) processes. In dsge each equation is an R formula. obs() marks a variable we observe, unobs() one we do not, and state() an exogenous state driven by a shock. lead(p) is the expectation of next quarter’s inflation.

nk <- dsge_model(
  obs(p   ~ beta * lead(p) + kappa * x),     # Phillips curve
  unobs(x ~ lead(x) - (r - lead(p) - g)),    # IS curve
  obs(r   ~ psi * p + u),                    # Taylor rule
  state(u ~ rhou * u),                       # monetary policy shock
  state(g ~ rhog * g),                       # demand shock
  start = list(beta = 0.95, kappa = 0.15, psi = 1.5, rhou = 0.7, rhog = 0.9)
)

The output gap is never observed. The Kalman filter infers it from the path of inflation and the interest rate.

The data

We need two quarterly series from FRED: inflation, computed from the GDP deflator, and the federal funds rate. FRED serves any series as a CSV file, so base R is enough to download them.

get_fred <- function(id, start, end) {
  url <- paste0("https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=", id,
                "&cosd=", start, "&coed=", end, "&fq=Quarterly&fam=avg")
  x <- read.csv(url)
  data.frame(date = as.Date(x[[1]]), value = as.numeric(x[[2]]))
}

deflator <- get_fred("GDPDEF",   "1954-10-01", "2015-12-31")
fedfunds <- get_fred("FEDFUNDS", "1955-01-01", "2015-12-31")

us <- data.frame(date = deflator$date[-1],
                 p = 400 * diff(log(deflator$value)))  # annualised inflation
us$r <- fedfunds$value[match(us$date, fedfunds$date)]
range(us$date)
#> [1] "1955-01-01" "2015-10-01"

That gives 244 quarters, from 1955 to 2015. dsge subtracts the sample means before estimation, so the model describes deviations from average inflation and the average interest rate.

Bayesian estimation

Priors are set with prior(). These say that households are patient (beta close to 1), that the Phillips curve is fairly flat (kappa small), that the Fed responds more than one for one to inflation (psi around 1.5), and that both shocks are persistent. The standard deviations of the shocks get a diffuse inverse-gamma prior by default.

priors <- list(
  beta  = prior("beta",  shape1 = 95,  shape2 = 5),
  kappa = prior("beta",  shape1 = 30,  shape2 = 70),
  psi   = prior("gamma", shape  = 184, rate   = 122.7),
  rhou  = prior("beta",  shape1 = 70,  shape2 = 20),
  rhog  = prior("beta",  shape1 = 70,  shape2 = 20)
)

bayes_dsge() samples the posterior with an adaptive random-walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. Four chains of 10,000 draws take about a minute on a laptop.

fit <- bayes_dsge(nk, data = us[, c("p", "r")], priors = priors,
                  chains = 4, iter = 10000, warmup = 5000, seed = 1)
summary(fit)

The summary reports effective sample sizes (ess), the Gelman-Rubin statistic (rhat) and the Monte Carlo standard error of each posterior mean (mcse). Values of rhat close to 1 mean the four chains agree.

Some results:

  • The estimated Taylor-rule coefficient psi is about 1.69, above 1: the Fed raised the nominal rate by more than the rise in inflation, so the real rate went up. This is the Taylor principle.
  • Demand shocks are more persistent than monetary policy shocks (rhog is about 0.93 against 0.65 for rhou).

Comparing prior and posterior shows how much the data have to say. The data pull the slope of the Phillips curve, kappa, well below its prior mean of 0.3, and push psi above its prior mean of 1.5:

plot(fit, type = "prior_posterior", pars = c("kappa", "psi"))

Impulse responses

What happens after a monetary tightening, or a jump in demand? irf() computes the responses at 500 posterior draws, and the plot shows the median response with credible bands.

irfs <- irf(fit, periods = 12, n_draws = 500)
plot(irfs, response = c("p", "r", "x"))

[UPLOAD IMAGE: dsge-irf-1.png]

A positive monetary policy shock (u) raises the interest rate, and the output gap and inflation fall. A positive demand shock (g) raises the output gap and inflation, and the Fed responds by raising the rate.

What drove US inflation?

shock_decomposition() uses the Kalman smoother to split each observed series, quarter by quarter, into the contributions of the two shocks, with the parameters at their posterior means. plot(hd) draws it with base graphics. If you use ggplot2, autoplot() returns a ggplot that you can modify, here to label the axis with years instead of quarter numbers:

hd <- shock_decomposition(fit)

library(ggplot2)
decades <- seq(1960, 2010, by = 10)
autoplot(hd, which = "p") +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = (decades - 1955) * 4 + 1, labels = decades) +
  labs(title = "What drove US inflation?", x = NULL)

[UPLOAD IMAGE: dsge-shock-decomposition-1.png]

The model’s reading of US history:

  • 1973 to 1980: inflation was well above average, and the two shocks share the blame about equally. Monetary policy shocks (u) added 1.9 points on average and demand shocks (g) 2.3 points. Monetary shocks push inflation up when interest rates are lower than the Taylor rule would set.
  • 1981 to 1984: the monetary shocks turn negative and take 2.1 points off inflation, while demand is still adding 3.9. This is the Volcker disinflation.
  • 2008 to 2015: weak demand holds inflation 2.7 points below average.

It is a small model with two shocks, so take this as an illustration rather than a verdict. It also ignores the zero lower bound on interest rates after 2008. dsge can handle constraints like that with simulate_occbin().

Nonlinear models

Linear models cover a lot of ground, but some questions need the nonlinear equations: risk, precautionary saving, or responses that depend on the size and sign of a shock. dsgenl_model() takes the equations as strings, with C(+1) for next period’s consumption. Here is a real business cycle model with capital K and technology Z:

rbc <- dsgenl_model(
  "1/C = beta / C(+1) * (alpha * exp(Z) * K^(alpha - 1) + 1 - delta)",
  "K(+1) = exp(Z) * K^alpha - C + (1 - delta) * K",
  "Z(+1) = rho * Z",
  observed = "C", endo_state = "K", exo_state = "Z",
  fixed = list(alpha = 0.33, beta = 0.99, delta = 0.025),
  start = list(rho = 0.9),
  ss_guess = c(C = 2, K = 30, Z = 0)
)

params <- c(alpha = 0.33, beta = 0.99, delta = 0.025, rho = 0.9)
steady_state(rbc, params = params)
#> Deterministic Steady State
#>   Converged: TRUE  ( 5  iterations)
#>   Max residual: 5.551e-17 
#> 
#>   Values:
#>      C = 2.306617 
#>      Z = 0 
#>      K = 28.34842

solve_dsge() finds the steady state, linearises around it and solves the model. With order = 2 it keeps the second-order terms of the decision rules, so a large negative shock no longer has the mirror-image effect of a large positive one:

sol2 <- solve_dsge(rbc, params = params, shock_sd = c(Z = 0.01), order = 2)

# Responses to technology shocks of plus and minus five standard deviations
up   <- irf_2nd_order(sol2, shock = "Z", size =  5, periods = 40)
down <- irf_2nd_order(sol2, shock = "Z", size = -5, periods = 40)

# Consumption on impact: it rises by more than it falls
c(up   = up$response[up$variable == "C"][1],
  down = down$response[down$variable == "C"][1])
#>          up        down 
#>  0.02285049 -0.02228800

plot(up) draws the second-order responses.

Third-order solutions (order = 3), the particle filter for estimating nonlinear models (bayes_particle()), perfect-foresight paths and occasionally binding constraints such as the zero lower bound (simulate_occbin()) are also available.

Bringing your Dynare files

Many published models come as Dynare .mod files. read_dynare() reads them, along with their calibration, shocks, priors and estimation settings. The result goes straight into solve_dsge(), irf() and the estimation functions. It takes a file path or, as here, the model code as text:

mod <- read_dynare(text = "
  var y pi i u;
  varexo e_d e_m;
  parameters beta sigma kappa phi_pi phi_y rho_i rho_u;
  beta = 0.99; sigma = 1; kappa = 0.1;
  phi_pi = 1.5; phi_y = 0.125; rho_i = 0.7; rho_u = 0.8;
  model(linear);
    y  = y(+1) - 1/sigma * (i - pi(+1)) + u;
    pi = beta * pi(+1) + kappa * y;
    i  = rho_i * i(-1) + (1 - rho_i) * (phi_pi * pi + phi_y * y) + e_m;
    u  = rho_u * u(-1) + e_d;
  end;
  shocks;
    var e_d; stderr 0.01;
    var e_m; stderr 0.0025;
  end;
")
plot(irf(solve_dsge(mod), periods = 20, se = FALSE),
     response = c("y", "pi", "i"))

[UPLOAD IMAGE: dsge-dynare-1.png]

How close is it to Dynare? For the Smets and Wouters (2007) model, the log-likelihood computed by dsge matches Dynare’s to nine decimal places.

What else is in the package

  • Maximum likelihood estimation with estimate(), with standard and robust standard errors
  • Sequential Monte Carlo (bayes_smc()), DSGE-VARs, and model comparison with Bayes factors (bayes_factor())
  • Forecasts and conditional forecasts (forecast(), conditional_forecast())
  • Variance decompositions, smoothed states and shocks
  • Identification and sensitivity diagnostics (check_identification(), parameter_sensitivity())
  • Optimal policy: Ramsey, discretion and optimal simple rules
  • autoplot() methods for ggplot2 users, and model_latex() to write a model’s equations in LaTeX
